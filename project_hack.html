Hackathon Web App Authentication Matching Team Project

Overview

BUHack is a 3-day university hackathon where teams build a web product to improve campus life. Unlike universities in mainland China, Hong Kong universities typically don't have fixed class groups or class committees — students' connections are weaker and more scattered. Our team focused on this gap and built a peer-matching web app that helps students find study partners, activity companions, and like-minded peers beyond their own department.

Our final product placed 7th overall. While we didn't make the top ranks, the experience gave me a clear picture of what a real hackathon demands — and what I would do differently next time.

The Problem

In Hong Kong universities, students take courses across departments with no fixed class structure. It's common to sit through a whole semester without knowing anyone in the room. Existing platforms (WhatsApp groups, Instagram, Discord) are informal and not designed for academic or interest-based matching.

We wanted to build something that makes it easy and low-pressure for students to find peers for study sessions, group projects, or shared hobbies.

What We Built

A full-stack web application with the following core features:

  • User authentication — Secure login and account system.
  • Matching flow — Users can browse and match with peers based on shared interests or study needs.
  • Posting system — Students can publish posts such as "looking for a study buddy for COMP3047".
  • Contact exchange — Once a match or application is accepted, the poster's contact information is shared, allowing them to continue the conversation privately.

The goal was to keep the interaction simple: post → match → connect.

Challenges & What Went Wrong

This was my first hackathon, and several factors affected our performance:

  • Time allocation: With only 3 days and no prior hackathon experience, our planning phase consumed too much of the schedule. We underestimated how long setup, debugging, and integration would take.
  • Team alignment: My teammates and I were grouped on the spot at the start of the event, so we had no prior understanding of each other's strengths, working styles, or tech stack preferences.
  • Task division: Because we didn't know each other's capabilities upfront, assigning roles took longer than expected — which directly cut into development time.
  • Scope: We tried to include authentication, matching, posting, and contact exchange — in hindsight, a smaller core feature set would have been more polished.

Outcome

  • Delivered a working full-stack web app within 3 days.
  • Placed 7th overall out of all competing teams.
  • Successfully demonstrated login, matching, posting, and contact exchange features in the final presentation.

What I Learned

  • Plan fast, build faster. In a hackathon, the first few hours should be about agreeing on scope and roles — not exploring options.
  • Know your team early. Pre-event communication (even a short chat about skills) would have saved significant time.
  • Cut scope, ship polished. A smaller feature set executed well beats a large one half-finished.
  • Ideas matter, but execution decides ranking. Our concept was solid; what held us back was pacing and coordination.

This experience made me much more aware of how to structure team projects — and I'm looking forward to applying these lessons in the next hackathon.
